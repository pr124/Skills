# Direction and composition

## Establish a design premise

Before styling, write a compact internal brief:

```text
Product:      what this is and what it enables
User/task:    who is here and what they need to do
Domain cues:  objects, materials, language, and visual references from its world
Voice:        2–3 precise traits, with an explicit counter-trait
Signature:    one repeatable element or behavior unique to this product
Rejecting:    3 likely template defaults and the chosen alternative
```

Examples of useful voice tension: `precise, calm, not sterile`; `playful, tactile, not childish`; `authoritative, editorial, not institutional`. Avoid empty labels such as "modern" or "clean" unless made concrete.

The signature can come from the product itself: a comparison rail for a research tool, a field-note motif for an outdoors brand, a live material swatch for an interiors shop, or an unusually useful timeline for a logistics product. It should improve recognition or understanding, not function as random decoration.

## Persuade register

Marketing and editorial pages need a narrative rather than a sequence of interchangeable sections.

- Build the page around a clear story: promise, evidence, mechanism, confidence, action. Reorder when the content demands it.
- Vary pacing. Pair dense evidence with open space; do not give every section the same height, alignment, and card treatment.
- Let one art-direction idea carry the page. Photography, illustration, type, texture, diagrams, or motion should share a world.
- Use asymmetric composition when it strengthens the reading path. Maintain alignment anchors so asymmetry feels intentional.
- Treat the hero as the first scene, not a mandatory left-copy/right-mockup component.
- Make proof product-specific: demonstrations, real screenshots, comparisons, examples, or methodology beat generic badge rows.

## Operate register

Applications and dashboards earn trust through clarity and consistency.

- Start from jobs and information architecture, not a gallery of cards.
- Use familiar navigation patterns unless a new pattern provides a measurable benefit.
- Match density to the task. Operational and data-heavy tools can be compact; low-frequency or guided tasks can breathe.
- Keep primary, secondary, and destructive actions visually distinct. One region should not contain several equal "primary" buttons.
- Prefer inline disclosure, split views, drawers, or contextual panels when they preserve working context.
- Give data shape: units, comparison baselines, time ranges, missing-data states, and aligned numbers. Do not add decorative charts.
- Use a stable grid and predictable component behavior. Personality should not make routine work harder.

## Read register

Documentation, guides, help centers, changelogs, and editorial surfaces succeed through sustained comprehension.

- Make wayfinding, search, table of contents, current location, and next steps obvious without crowding the reading column.
- Treat type hierarchy, line length, code blocks, tables, figures, citations, and callouts as a coordinated reading system.
- Keep navigation stable while allowing the article body to breathe. Do not turn every paragraph into a card or callout.
- Use progressive disclosure for advanced detail while keeping prerequisites and critical warnings in the main path.
- Design for scanning and deep reading: descriptive headings, meaningful anchors, useful summaries, and clear examples.

## Experience register

Portfolios, galleries, immersive stories, and showcases are interfaces around an artifact.

- Let the work dominate the visual field; chrome should recede until needed.
- Use pacing, crop, scale, sequencing, and transition to frame the artifact rather than decorating around it.
- Keep orientation and escape routes clear even when the composition is experimental.
- Protect media quality and performance. A dramatic concept that loads slowly or obscures the work defeats itself.
- Ensure keyboard, touch, reduced-motion, and alternative-text behavior survive the immersive treatment.

## Composition rules

- Create hierarchy with the fewest necessary variables: position, space, scale, weight, and color.
- Group by proximity before adding containers, dividers, or background fills.
- Use a spacing rhythm with meaningful contrast. Uniform medium gaps make hierarchy flat.
- Avoid endless full-width bands and identical card grids. Change structure when the content relationship changes.
- Constrain text measure independently from the outer layout. Wide screens do not require wide paragraphs.
- Use content-driven breakpoints. Responsive design is re-composition, not proportional shrinking.
- On large screens, cap readable and operational regions rather than stretching every control to fill 4K.
- On narrow screens, preserve core actions and reflow relationships; do not simply hide important functionality.

## Variant discipline

When the user requests variants, vary one primary decision at a time: structure, density, emphasis, type voice, or interaction model. Three versions with different accent colors are not meaningful exploration. Keep all variants above the same accessibility and product-quality floor.
